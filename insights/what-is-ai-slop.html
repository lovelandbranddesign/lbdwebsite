What Is AI Slop?

August 18, 2026
In This Post
AI slop is content generic enough to describe any business. What it actually means, why it erodes trust, and what real specificity looks like instead.
← All Insights
Start My MAP

Short answer: AI slop is marketing or media content, written by a person or a machine, that is generic enough to describe almost any business in its category. It has no real names, no real numbers, no checkable claims, and no specific story. Merriam-Webster named "slop" its 2025 Word of the Year, defining it as low-quality digital content produced in quantity through artificial intelligence. The term caught on because everyone already recognized the pattern. AI just made it faster to produce.

The definition, in plain terms

AI slop is content that could belong to anyone.

Read a sentence like "nestled in the heart of the mountains, we exude warmth and welcome guests with open arms," and try to name the business it describes. You cannot. It could be a pizza restaurant, a bed and breakfast, a distillery, or a law firm. The sentence carries no information specific to the business writing it. That interchangeability is the actual definition. Not the writing quality. Not whether a machine wrote it. Whether it says anything true and specific that no other business could claim.

Slop shows up in five recognizable patterns:

  1. Generic, templated phrasing. Language built from stock marketing phrases instead of real detail about the business.
  2. Vague or unattributed claims. "Voted best in the region" with no source. "Our clients love us" with no name attached.
  3. No named human behind the words. No author, no founder, no specific person taking ownership of what is written.
  4. Repeated structure. The same paragraph, or a close variant of it, appearing more than once on a single page.
  5. Absence of verifiable detail. No real numbers, no real dates, no real names that a reader could check.

A page can fail all five criteria or just one. The more it fails, the more it reads as slop.

Where the term came from

"Slop" entered common use as shorthand for low-effort AI output well before Merriam-Webster made it official. The dictionary's selection in late 2025 reflected a pattern already visible everywhere: social feeds filling with AI-generated images that resembled nothing real, marketing copy that repeated the same three adjectives across a thousand unrelated businesses, and a general public that had gotten fast at spotting it.

Google Trends data confirms the timing. Search interest in "AI slop" spiked hard around the Word of the Year announcement and settled into a sustained baseline well above where it started. This is not a term that trended and disappeared. People are still actively searching for it, still trying to understand what it means for their own business or their own content.

Why it matters beyond annoyance

Slop is not just an aesthetic problem. It carries three real costs.

It erodes trust. A reader who spots one generic, unverifiable claim starts questioning every claim on the page. Trust does not partially survive contact with obvious filler.

It fails at the exact moment AI answer engines are choosing what to cite. Systems like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI Overviews increasingly summarize answers instead of sending a click. Princeton research on Generative Engine Optimization found that keyword stuffing and generic phrasing actually score below baseline for AI visibility. The systems built to synthesize the internet's content are penalizing exactly the pattern slop represents.

It wastes the real story sitting underneath it. In an audit of small business websites across Eastern Kentucky, several businesses with genuinely compelling, verifiable histories opened their own homepage with the same interchangeable line. A distillery founded by immigrants with a specific, documented story led with "nestled in the Appalachian foothills," a sentence that could describe any business in any mountain range in the country. The real story existed. It just was not being told.

What replaces it

The fix is not more polish or a bigger budget. Two of the least slop-affected sites in the same audit had no professional design at all: a lake marina running plain, functional copy, and a media arts nonprofit operating out of a rented office above a Dollar General. Both scored better than well-funded, professionally built sites simply because what they said was specific and true.

Replacing slop means:

  • A real name instead of "our team"
  • A real number instead of "affordable" or "industry-leading"
  • A real story instead of "rich heritage" or "proud tradition"
  • One clear, checkable claim instead of three vague ones

None of that costs more to produce. It requires telling the truth about what makes a business different, instead of reaching for language that fits any business in the category.

Frequently asked questions

Is AI slop the same as AI-generated content?

No. AI slop describes a pattern of generic, unverifiable, interchangeable language. That pattern existed in marketing copy long before generative AI tools were widely available. AI made it cheaper and faster to produce at scale, but it did not invent the pattern, and not everything AI-assisted qualifies as slop.

Can a human write AI slop without using AI at all?

Yes. A tired copywriter reaching for the same stock phrases used across a thousand other websites produces slop regardless of what tool they used. The term describes the output, not the process that created it.

How do I know if my own website has AI slop on it?

Read your homepage and ask one question of every sentence: could this describe a competitor just as easily? If the answer is yes, it is slop. If the answer is no, because the sentence contains a real name, a real number, or a detail unique to your business, it is not.

Does fixing AI slop require hiring an agency?

No. It requires specificity, which any business owner can supply about their own business better than an outside writer can invent it. What often helps is a second, honest set of eyes checking whether the specifics actually made it onto the page, rather than staying in the owner's head.
