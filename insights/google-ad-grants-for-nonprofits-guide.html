Google Ad Grants for Nonprofits: The Complete 2026 Guide

October 27, 2026
In This Post
Google Ad Grants gives eligible 501(c)(3) nonprofits up to $10,000 a month in Search ads. The rules, real results, honest limits, and how to stay compliant.
← All Insights
Start the Conversation

Short answer: Google Ad Grants gives eligible 501(c)(3) nonprofits up to $10,000 a month in free Google Search advertising, which equals $329 a day. Google calls that figure a limit on spend, and the amount an account uses depends on keywords, bids, ad quality, and search demand. The grant pays for the clicks. The work is keeping the account compliant, tracking real conversions, and sending visitors to a website that earns their action. This guide covers who qualifies, every rule that keeps an account active, what documented results look like, the honest numbers, and where the program falls short.

What the Google Ad Grant is

The Google Ad Grant is a donated-advertising program. Eligible nonprofits run text ads on Google Search, and Google covers the cost up to the monthly cap. Google donated its first Search ads to the March of Dimes in 2003. In a March 1, 2023 post, Google reported that the program donated more than $1.8 billion worth of Search ads in 2022 alone, has driven more than 14 billion clicks to nonprofit websites, and serves over 65,000 nonprofits that each receive up to $10,000 in donated Search ads every month.

Those totals describe the program. Individual results vary widely, and the sections below separate what Google documents from what the sector reports.

Who qualifies

The organization must be a US nonprofit recognized by the IRS under Section 501(c)(3), or a group exempt organization with proven affiliation to a central 501(c)(3). Every applicant is verified through Google for Nonprofits and its validation partner Goodstack. TechSoup stopped performing new validations for Google for Nonprofits on August 21, 2023, and the partner Percent rebranded as Goodstack on October 15, 2024.

Google excludes three categories: governmental entities and organizations, hospitals and health care organizations, and schools, academic institutions, and universities. Two exceptions matter. Charitable arms and foundations of health care organizations are eligible, and so are the philanthropic arms of educational organizations. Fiscally sponsored organizations without their own 501(c)(3) status are currently not eligible.

Verification comes first. Google states that most Google for Nonprofits requests are reviewed in 2 to 14 business days. After verification, the Ad Grants activation review may take several business days. Google instructs organizations not to create a Google Ads account at any point during the activation process.

Website requirements

Google reviews the website before it approves ads, and the same standards stay in force after approval. The documented requirements are:

  • The organization owns the domain its ads point to, and the domain does not redirect visitors to a third-party platform such as a donation portal or a social media page.
  • Every page is served over HTTPS.
  • The site shows a clear mission and substantial original content.
  • Donation links work and lead to a secure page dedicated to donations.
  • The site runs no AdSense ads and no excessive third-party ads.
  • Pages load fast and work on mobile.

Google also recommends showing a physical address, the nonprofit registration number, and an annual report. A site that fails these standards delays approval and weakens every campaign that follows, which is why website repair comes before the grant application.

The rules that keep an account active

Approval is the first gate. Staying approved is the ongoing work. These are the requirements Google publishes for accounts in good standing.

Google adds one enforcement rule that explains why unmanaged accounts decay: any account found in violation of program policies is subject to automatic suspension without notification. Reinstatement runs through a request form after the issues are fixed.

One detail about conversions deserves attention. Time on site and homepage visits can be tracked, but Google requires them to be categorized as Other and excluded from Conversions. A real conversion is an action that matters to the mission.

What documented results look like

The examples below come from three kinds of sources. Google reports the first group. Agencies report the second group, and each carries a vendor-reported label because the agency selected the example and the numbers have no independent audit.

Reported by Google

Penny Lane (Los Angeles; foster care, residential care, and youth mental health). In 2022, its Ad Grants campaigns helped the organization recruit 64 volunteers and raise more than $20,000 in donations.

Elder Wisdom Circle (Walnut Creek, California; intergenerational advice). Over a decade, Ad Grants led to nearly 500,000 requests for advice. In 2022, 76% of the organization’s website visitors originated from ads.

immunize.org (vaccine education). Traffic grew from 25,000 visitors per day to over 40,000 at the peak of its vaccination program.

Source for all three: Jerry Dischler, Google, March 1, 2023.

Vendor-reported

All Hands and Hearts (disaster relief). Community Boost reports $15,000 in donations over 90 days and a 109% increase in conversions after moving to conversion-based bidding.

Moms In Prayer International (faith-based ministry). Community Boost reports tracking donations, email subscriptions, and prayer-group sign-ups, with $17,000 in donations in 90 days.

An unnamed charity (Digital Charity Lab, February 2019). After the January 2018 policy changes, the charity compared 2017 with 2018. Impressions fell 84% and clicks fell 60%. Click-through rate rose from 4.93% to 12.44%, and donations rose from 97 to 157, an increase of 62%. The lesson: stricter rules push an account toward fewer, better-matched visitors.

Three patterns run through every example. Each organization tracked an action that mattered to its mission. Each pointed ads at a page built for that action. Each managed the account every month after launch.

The honest numbers

The most complete independent benchmark comes from M+R, whose Benchmarks 2026 study measured Google Ad Grants against paid search:

  • Average return on ad spend for Google Grants was $0.17, compared with $2.48 for paid search.
  • The average cost per donation for Google Grants was $793.
  • Nonprofits received 229 site visits for every $1,000 in grant spend.
  • Grant placements appear below paid results.

M+R adds a caution that belongs next to those figures: return on ad spend is a bit deceptive here, because no actual money is spent. A $793 cost per donation is a notional figure in grant dollars. The organization pays nothing for the clicks.

The data supports a clear plan. Budget the grant as a source of visits, volunteers, sign-ups, program enrollment, and local visibility. Treat donation revenue as a bonus. A common claim in the sector says the average grant account spends about $300 a month. That figure traces to a 2014 conference slide deck and carries no Google source, so treat it as unverified.

Six situations and what they call for

These situations come up repeatedly under Google’s published rules. They illustrate how the rules apply. They are not client case studies.

A nonprofit with no conversion tracking

For accounts created on or after April 22, 2019, a valid conversion each month is a requirement, and Smart Bidding has no signal to optimize without one. The fix is tracking through Google Analytics 4 or the Google Ads tag, with actions chosen for the mission, followed by a reinstatement request if the account was deactivated.

A hospital-affiliated organization

A hospital is ineligible. A separately registered charitable foundation associated with the hospital qualifies. The foundation applies under its own name and 501(c)(3) status, with its own website.

A fiscally sponsored group

Without its own 501(c)(3) determination, the group is not eligible today. The path forward is the IRS determination, after which the normal verification steps apply.

An account spending $300 of $10,000

Google lists the causes: keywords that are too specific or have low search volume, bids that are not competitive, low ad quality or landing page experience, and narrow targeting. Smart Bidding also does not spend when it sees no opportunity for conversions. The fixes are keyword expansion within the rules, stronger landing pages, and valid conversions.

An account below 5% click-through rate

Google’s documented fix starts with a seven-day date range, sorting keywords by impressions, and pausing the high-impression, low-click-through keywords until the filtered account rate reads at least 5%. Then the organization requests reactivation.

An unmanaged account

Keywords get paused automatically without notice. Survey emails go unanswered. The click-through rate drifts. Eventually the account is deactivated. Idealist volunteer postings show the visible end of this pattern: a Pennsylvania nonprofit reported that its approved donation campaigns were receiving zero impressions, and a Maryland nonprofit posted in January 2026 that its account had zero clicks and fewer than 20 impressions.

The first 90 days

  1. Repair the website. Confirm the owned domain, sitewide HTTPS, no redirects, a working donation page, and substantial original content.
  2. Register and verify. Apply through Google for Nonprofits and wait for Goodstack verification.
  3. Activate Ad Grants. Submit the activation request and create no separate Google Ads account.
  4. Accept both invitations. Google sends an account invitation and a payments profile invitation, and the payments profile carries no charge.
  5. Set up conversion tracking. Connect Google Analytics 4 and Google Ads, and define the actions that matter.
  6. Build the account to the rules. Use specific location targeting, at least two ad groups per campaign, at least two sitelinks, and multi-word mission keywords.
  7. Move to conversion-based bidding. Google advises switching from Maximize clicks once the account has enough data, typically 15 to 30 conversions in the last 30 days.
  8. Review monthly. Check click-through rate, conversions, paused keywords, and the program survey.

Performance Max campaigns are also available in Ad Grants accounts. In June 2025, Google expanded Ad Grants to eligible Google Maps placements in Performance Max campaigns.

The grant pays for the clicks. The compliance, the tracking, and the website decide what those clicks are worth.

The honest limits

  • Search only. The grant covers Google Search text ads, plus Maps through Performance Max. Display, YouTube, and remarketing need a separate paid account.
  • Lower placement. Grant ads appear below paid results, so highly competitive terms are out of reach.
  • A daily ceiling. Unused budget does not roll over, and an event or crisis surge hits the $329 daily limit.
  • Keyword restrictions. Generic single words, other organizations’ names, and low-quality keywords are restricted or paused.
  • Lower donation efficiency. The M+R figures above show the gap against paid search.
  • Search is changing. M+R reported in November 2025 that in a sample of 17 nonprofits, visitors from organic search fell 13% year over year in August, which raises the value of any channel that still delivers qualified visits.

The grant fits poorly when an organization has no content-rich website it owns, no capacity to track conversions, or a mission with very little search volume. For those organizations, the first investment belongs in the website and the tracking, and the grant follows.

Questions to ask before you hire anyone for the grant

  1. Who owns the Google Ads account and the login?
  2. Which conversions will you track, and how do they connect to our mission?
  3. How will you report click-through rate and conversions each month?
  4. What happens when Google pauses keywords or sends a program survey?
  5. Is the price fixed, and what does it include?

An agency with specific answers has a real process. An agency that answers with generalities is asking you to trust the account to luck.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Google Ad Grant really free?

Yes. The grant covers the cost of your Search ads, up to $10,000 a month. Google sends a payments profile invitation during setup and states that you will not be charged. The cost to a nonprofit is the work: a compliant website, conversion tracking, and monthly management.

Which nonprofits qualify for Google Ad Grants?

US organizations recognized under Section 501(c)(3), including group exemptions affiliated with a central 501(c)(3), and verified through Google for Nonprofits and its partner Goodstack. Governmental entities, hospitals and health care organizations, and schools and universities are not eligible, though charitable arms and foundations of those institutions can qualify.

How long does approval take?

Google states that most Google for Nonprofits verification requests are reviewed in 2 to 14 business days. The Ad Grants activation review that follows may take several business days, according to Google's help pages.

Why do Google Ad Grants accounts get deactivated?

The documented causes include an account click-through rate below 5% for two consecutive months, no valid conversion in a month, and an unanswered program survey. Google also states that any account found in violation of program policies is subject to automatic suspension without notification.

Can a nonprofit raise donations with Google Ad Grants?

Some do. Google reports that Penny Lane raised more than $20,000 in donations in 2022 through its Ad Grants campaigns. Across 2026 benchmark data, however, grant clicks convert to donations far less efficiently than paid search clicks, so the strongest uses are volunteers, sign-ups, program enrollment, and awareness.

Can a fiscally sponsored organization get the grant?

Not today. Google lists fiscally sponsored organizations without their own 501(c)(3) status as currently not eligible. The organization needs its own IRS determination first.
